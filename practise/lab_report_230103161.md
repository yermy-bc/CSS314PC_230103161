## 2. Phase 1 — False-Sharing Stride Sweep & Karp–Flatt

M = 1.6·10⁷ RMW steps on `uint64` slots in a 4096-byte `SharedMemory` block. Worker *w* updates slot *w·stride*.

### 2.1 Stride sweep (p = P_phys = 4)

| stride | offset (B) | median (ms) | IQR (ms) | min (ms) | Mops/s |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 8 | 1305.67 | 196.32 | 1250.73 | 12.25 |
| 2 | 16 | 1348.61 | 505.07 | 1264.37 | 11.86 |
| 4 | 32 | 1348.80 | 168.38 | 1233.94 | 11.86 |
| 8 | **64** | 1168.69 | 192.92 | 1033.84 | 13.69 |
| 16 | **128** | 1028.47 | 184.60 | 955.42 | 15.56 |
| 32 | 256 | 1039.56 | 146.94 | 941.10 | 15.39 |

**Where contention drops.** Strides of 8–32 B put all 4 counters in one 64-B line, and throughput stays flat at about 12 Mops/s. The first sharp drop (−13.4 %) comes at a **64-byte** offset, where every counter gets its own cache line. A second drop (−12 %) comes at **128 B**, and beyond that the curve is flat (256 B ≈ 128 B). The 128-B step matches the Intel L2 *spatial (adjacent-line) prefetcher*, which fetches lines in 128-B aligned pairs. Neighbouring lines therefore still interfere at 64 B, and only a 128-B separation removes all coherence traffic. Overall, the false-sharing penalty at p = 4 is T(stride 1)/T(stride 16) = **1.27×**.

The penalty is only 27 % because each Python RMW step costs about 80 ns of interpreter work. A cache-line transfer between cores (MESI invalidate + RFO, roughly 50–100 cycles ≈ 20 ns) is only a fraction of that. In C, the same kernel would show a 5–10× gap.

### 2.2 Process scaling (S_p = T₁/T_p, E_p = S_p/p, Karp–Flatt e(p))

| p | T_p s=1 (ms) | S_p | E_p | **e(p) s=1** | T_p s=16 (ms) | S_p | E_p | **e(p) s=16** |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3084.27 | 1.000 | 1.000 | – | 2933.64 | 1.000 | 1.000 | – |
| 2 | 1803.62 | 1.710 | 0.855 | 0.1696 | 1560.40 | 1.880 | 0.940 | 0.0638 |
| 4 | 1305.67 | 2.362 | 0.591 | 0.2311 | 1028.47 | 2.852 | 0.713 | 0.1341 |
| 8 | 970.15 | 3.179 | 0.397 | 0.2166 | 950.97 | 3.085 | 0.386 | 0.2276 |

### 2.3 Analysis

The kernel has no true serial section, so any non-zero e(p) is *parallel overhead*.

* **stride = 1 (false sharing).** At p = 2 we already have e = 0.17, rising to 0.23 at p = 4. With every core writing into the same line, ownership moves between cores on almost every store, and the stores effectively serialize through the coherence protocol. e(p) is large from the start and grows with p because more cores compete for the same line.
* **stride = 16 (padded, 128 B).** e = 0.064 at p = 2 and 0.13 at p = 4, which is roughly **2–2.7× lower**. The remaining overhead does not come from coherence. It comes from the turbo clock dropping as more cores become active (the i5-11300H turbos higher on one core than on four) and from Windows scheduling noise.
* **p = 8 (crossing into SMT).** The two curves meet (0.217 vs 0.228). Two hyper-threads on one core share its L1d, so false sharing between SMT siblings stays inside the core and causes no cross-core invalidations. That is why stride 1 gains from p = 4 to p = 8 (1306 → 970 ms). For padded data, however, SMT adds only 8 % (S: 2.85 → 3.09) because the interpreter loop already keeps the core's ports busy. In both cases e(8) is about 0.22, and the cause is the *hardware*: 4 cores, not 8. A Karp–Flatt value that rises with p (instead of staying flat, which would indicate a true serial fraction) is the signature of growing overhead, from contention or from oversubscribing physical cores.

## 3. Phase 2 — Strong vs Weak Scaling

Stage A runs serially in the parent on 0.05·N elements (10 sin/sqrt ops per element). Stage B runs on a persistent `mp.Pool(p)` over N elements in p contiguous chunks (30 FMA/trig ops per element, no shared writes).

### 3.1 Strong scaling (N = 1.2·10⁶)

s = T_A(1)/T₁ = 22.8 / 1286.6 = **0.0177** ⇒ Amdahl cap **S∞ = 1/s = 56.5**

| p | T_p (ms) | IQR | S_p | E_p | Amdahl S(p) | ΔS (marginal) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1286.6 | 361.7 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | – |
| 2 | 641.5 | 108.9 | 2.006 | 1.003 | 1.965 | +1.006 |
| 3 | 466.0 | 87.5 | 2.761 | 0.920 | 2.897 | +0.755 |
| 4 | 341.2 | 33.6 | **3.771** | 0.943 | 3.798 | +1.010 |
| 5 | 381.0 | 60.4 | 3.377 | 0.675 | 4.670 | **−0.394** |
| 6 | 361.4 | 19.9 | 3.560 | 0.593 | 5.512 | +0.183 |
| 7 | 352.9 | 8.9 | 3.645 | 0.521 | 6.328 | +0.085 |
| 8 | 329.7 | 12.3 | **3.903** | 0.488 | 7.118 | +0.257 |

Up to p = P_phys = 4, the measurements follow Amdahl's law closely (3.77 measured vs 3.80 predicted, E ≈ 0.94). Past 4, the speedup plateaus near 3.4–3.9. **S_strong(8) = 3.90 is only 6.9 % of the Amdahl cap.** The limit here is not the 1.8 % serial fraction. It is the hardware: there are only 4 physical cores.

### 3.2 Weak scaling (N₀ = 2·10⁵, N(p) = p·N₀)

| p | N | T_p (ms) | IQR | E_weak = T₁/T_p | α = T_A(p)/T_p | S_Gustafson = p − α(p−1) | measured scaled speedup p·E_weak |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 200 000 | 227.0 | 77.6 | 1.000 | 0.0167 | 1.000 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 400 000 | 168.5 | 57.7 | 1.347 | 0.0441 | 1.956 | 2.69 |
| 3 | 600 000 | 223.8 | 42.9 | 1.015 | 0.0498 | 2.900 | 3.05 |
| 4 | 800 000 | 244.5 | 15.0 | 0.929 | 0.0614 | 3.816 | 3.72 |
| 5 | 1 000 000 | 323.5 | 20.3 | 0.702 | 0.0624 | 4.750 | 3.51 |
| 6 | 1 200 000 | 395.6 | 83.8 | 0.574 | 0.0648 | 5.676 | 3.44 |
| 7 | 1 400 000 | 417.0 | 14.7 | 0.544 | 0.0686 | 6.588 | 3.81 |
| 8 | 1 600 000 | 442.1 | 13.4 | 0.513 | 0.0741 | **7.481** | 4.10 |

Weak efficiency stays at or above 0.93 through p = 4. It then falls to about 0.5 at p = 8, because 8 equal tasks share 4 cores and each task takes about twice as long. E_weak(2) > 1 is a measurement artifact. The p = 1 baseline has a very large IQR (78 ms on 227 ms) from turbo ramp-up and Windows timer jitter, so T₁ is inflated. α grows from 0.017 to 0.074 because Stage A grows with N(p) and runs on one core, while T_p is held back by the oversubscribed cores.

**Gustafson vs reality.** Gustafson's formula gives S = 7.48 at p = 8 because it assumes the parallel part runs at p× rate. The speedup the hardware actually delivers is only 8 × 0.513 = **4.1**. Weak scaling hides a serial fraction well, but it cannot create cores that do not exist.

### 3.3 Architectural inflection (P_phys → P_phys + 1 = 4 → 5)

| | value |
|---|---|
| ΔS at p = 4 (last physical core) | **+1.010** |
| ΔS at p = 5 (first SMT thread) | **−0.394** |
| Drop in marginal speedup | **1.40** (the marginal gain becomes negative) |

The i5-11300H has no E-cores, so this boundary is the **Hyper-Threading boundary**. Stage B is split *statically* into p equal chunks. At p = 5, one core has to run two chunks on its two hyper-threads. Each of those threads gets only about 55–60 % of a core, so that core's chunk takes about (1/5)/0.6 ≈ 1/3 of the serial time, and the job takes as long as its slowest chunk: S ≈ 3.3, which matches the measured 3.38. As p rises toward 8 the imbalance shrinks, because every core ends up with two threads, and S recovers to 3.90. The total SMT gain is therefore only 3.90 / 3.77 ≈ **+3.5 %**. The CPython interpreter loop is bound by instruction throughput and by register/stack traffic, and it leaves few idle execution slots for a second thread to fill.

## 4. Phase 3 — Empirical Roofline & Cache-Blocked GEMM

### 4.1 Part 3A — Roofline ceilings (p = 4)

| Ceiling | Measurement | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **BW_sustained** | parallel `mv_a[s:e] = mv_b[s:e]` copy of 64 MB (>> 8 MiB L3), median 9.93 ms (IQR 1.82) | **12.89 GB/s** = 2·64·10⁶ / (T·10⁹) |
| **P_sustained** | 16-accumulator scalar FMA loop, 6.0·10⁷ FLOP/worker × 4, median 1010.5 ms | **0.2375 GFLOP/s** |
| **Ridge point I\*** | P / BW | **0.0184 FLOP/byte** |

* The memoryview slice copy is a C `memcpy`, so it does reach DRAM speed. 12.9 GB/s counts only 2 bytes moved per byte copied. With write-allocate (a read-for-ownership on the destination), real traffic is 3× the copy size, about 19 GB/s. That is a typical sustained figure for laptop DDR4-3200 (theoretical dual-channel peak 51.2 GB/s).
* The compute ceiling is set by the **interpreter**, not by the FPU. Each `x = x*a + b` executes about 6 bytecodes and boxes a new float object. The result is about 0.06 GFLOP/s per core, compared with roughly 64 GFLOP/s per core native peak (1 × 512-bit FMA unit × 8 doubles × 2 FLOP × ~4 GHz). That makes the Python loop about **1000× below** the silicon.
* Because P is so low, the ridge point is only **I\* ≈ 0.018 FLOP/B**. Any Python kernel with I > 0.02, which includes all three GEMM variants, sits on the flat *compute (interpreter) roof* and never touches the memory slope.

### 4.2 Part 3B — Progressive GEMM (N = 256, double, 1.57 MB, p = 4)

GFLOP/s = 2N³ / (T·10⁹), with 2N³ = 33.55 MFLOP.

| Variant | median (ms) | IQR | GFLOP/s | W_tile = 3·BS²·8 | I ≈ BS/12 | max\|ΔC\| |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| V1 naive i-j-k | 585.9 | 66.5 | **0.05727** | – | – | reference (spot-check vs pure-Python dot products 2.2e-15) |
| V2 reordered i-k-j | 716.1 | 134.1 | 0.04686 | – | – | 0.0 vs V1 |
| V3 BS = 8 | 830.9 | 197.6 | 0.04039 | 1.5 KiB | 0.67 | 0.0 |
| V3 BS = 16 | 686.9 | 42.4 | 0.04885 | 6.0 KiB | 1.33 | 0.0 |
| V3 BS = 32 | 707.3 | 122.7 | 0.04744 | 24 KiB | 2.67 | 0.0 |
| V3 BS = 64 | 684.5 | 155.4 | 0.04902 | 96 KiB | 5.33 | 0.0 |
| **V3 BS\* = 96** | **655.2** | 84.5 | **0.05121** | **216 KiB** | **8.00** | 0.0 |
| V3 BS = 128 | 714.3 | 63.4 | 0.04698 | 384 KiB | 10.67 | 0.0 |

Verification: max|C⁽³⁾ − C⁽²⁾| = 0 < 10⁻⁹ for every BS, so the blocked kernel is bit-identical to the reordered one.

### 4.3 Analysis

**BS\* vs cache sizes.** BS\* = 96 gives W_tile = 216 KiB. That is larger than L1d (48 KiB per core) but fits well inside the per-core L2 (1.25 MiB). The tiles that fit in L1d are BS ≤ 32 (24 KiB); BS = 64 (96 KiB) already spills out of L1d. In a compiled kernel the best tile would sit in L1d (BS ≈ 32–44). In Python the best tile is "as large as possible while staying in L2", for two reasons:

1. All three matrices (1.57 MB) almost fit in L2 + L3 anyway. Each worker touches its 64 rows of A and C (2 × 128 KiB) plus all of B (512 KiB), about 768 KiB, which is less than 1.25 MiB of L2. So cache misses are rare for *every* variant.
2. A memoryview element access costs about 20–40 ns of interpreter work, far more than an L1-vs-L2 latency difference (about 1 ns vs 4 ns). The variants therefore differ mainly in **bytecode count per FLOP**, not in cache behaviour. Small tiles (BS = 8) are slowest because the six-loop structure creates many short `range()` objects and `min()` calls per FLOP. Large tiles amortize that overhead, up to BS = 128, where the tile no longer divides the 64 rows each worker owns and the edge tiles waste work.

**Why V2 < V1 here (the opposite of a C implementation).** V1 keeps its running sum in a local Python float (`s += A[..]*B[..]`), which costs 2 buffer loads per FMA. V2 does `C[iN+j] += a_ik*B[kN+j]`, which costs a buffer load, a buffer store and a float box/unbox for C on every FMA: 3 buffer operations instead of 2, so it is about 20 % slower. The unit-stride benefit of V2 is real in hardware, but it is worth about 1 ns per access and is hidden behind roughly 30 ns of interpreter work per access. V3 recovers 9 % over V2 (V3/V2 = 1.09×) through better loop amortization, but stays below V1 (0.89×).

**Roofline mapping** (attainable = min(P, I·BW), with I\* = 0.018):

| Variant | Operational intensity I (FLOP/B) | Attainable (GFLOP/s) | Measured | % of P | Region |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| V1 | ≈ 0.125 (strided B: 8 B per 2 FLOP after A reuse) | 0.2375 | 0.0573 | 24 % | right of ridge → compute/interpreter bound |
| V2 | ≈ 0.25 (B streamed once per row) | 0.2375 | 0.0469 | 20 % | compute/interpreter bound |
| V3 (BS\*=96) | ≈ 8.0 | 0.2375 | 0.0512 | 22 % | compute/interpreter bound |

All three variants sit far to the right of the tiny ridge point, under the horizontal roof, at 20–24 % of the FMA-loop ceiling. They land below that ceiling because GEMM needs extra index arithmetic and buffer reads per FLOP, while the FMA loop uses register-like locals. If the same code ran natively (P ≈ 250 GFLOP/s, I\* ≈ 20 FLOP/B), V1 and V2 would fall on the memory-bound slope and blocking (I ∝ BS) would be what moves the kernel toward the compute roof. This is the textbook result, and the Python implementation cannot show it.

## 5. Phase 4 — Skewed Workload & Dynamic Chunk Queue

N = 8000 items, K(i) = 1 + ⌊(i/40)²⌋ (1 … 39 991 iterations of sin/cos/sqrt), 1.067·10⁸ iterations in total, p = 4.
λ = max_t t_worker[t] / mean_t t_worker[t].

| Strategy | T_wall (ms) | IQR | λ | t_worker[0..3] (s) | Σ t_worker (s) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|
| Contiguous block static | **10025.6** | 1285.3 | **2.154** | 0.33 / 2.34 / 5.92 / 10.03 | 18.6 |
| Cyclic round-robin static | 5189.1 | 96.3 | 1.063 | 4.92 / 4.60 / 4.82 / 5.19 | 19.5 |
| Dynamic C = 1 | **4578.8** | 130.1 | **1.001** | 4.57 / 4.58 / 4.57 / 4.58 | 18.3 |
| Dynamic C = 8 | 5557.9 | 120.7 | 1.003 | 5.53 / 5.52 / 5.55 / 5.56 | 22.2 |
| Dynamic C = 64 | 5421.6 | 311.2 | 1.034 | 5.23 / 5.24 / 5.42 / 5.09 | 21.0 |
| Dynamic C = 512 | 6235.0 | 296.7 | 1.115 | 6.23 / 6.08 / 4.52 / 5.55 | 22.4 |

**Optimal chunk C\* = 1.** T_wall(dynamic, C\*) = 4578.8 ms, a **2.19× speedup** over block static.

### Analysis

* **Block static.** Work grows as i², so the last quarter of the items (6000–7999) holds 1 − (3/4)³ = 58 % of all work. The analytically ideal λ is 2.313, and we measured **2.154**. Worker 0 finishes after 0.33 s and then idles for 9.7 s. T_wall is set entirely by worker 3.
* **Cyclic static** interleaves heavy and light items, so each worker gets nearly the same mix (λ = 1.063). The remaining 6 % comes from worker t always receiving slightly heavier items than worker t − 1 in every round.
* **C = 1 vs C = 512 (contention vs tail).**
  * C = 1 means 8000 lock acquisitions on `mp.Value('i')`. On Windows each acquisition is a kernel semaphore operation costing about 5–20 µs, so the total is ≤ 160 ms, spread across 4 workers, which is under 1 % of the run. Contention only matters when the lock cost is close to the per-item cost. Here the *average* item runs 13 300 iterations (≈ 1.7 ms), about 100× the lock cost, and even the lightest items are cheap enough that collisions are rare. In exchange, C = 1 gives a perfect tail: the last item handed out is at most one item (≤ 5 ms) away from the end, so **λ = 1.001**.
  * C = 512 needs only 16 lock operations, but the final chunk (items 7680–7999) alone holds **11.5 % of the total work**, about 2.1 s of single-core time. Whichever worker takes that chunk finishes far behind the others (t_worker = 4.52 s vs 6.23 s), giving λ = 1.115 and the slowest dynamic time.
  * C = 8 and C = 64 sit between the two (λ ≈ 1.00–1.03). Note that their *Σ t_worker* rose to 21–22 s against 18.3 s for C = 1, even though the work is identical. That increase is not scheduling overhead. These configurations ran later in the sequence, after more than 2 minutes of full 4-core load, and the laptop's all-core turbo clock had dropped (thermal / power-limit throttling). λ is a ratio, so it is unaffected by the clock and shows that imbalance stays low for those chunk sizes.
* **Choice of C\*.** For this workload the per-item cost is much larger than the synchronization cost, so the smallest chunk wins: **C\* = 1** (lowest T_wall, λ ≈ 1). As a general rule, C should be the smallest chunk whose lock overhead stays negligible relative to chunk compute time. With finer-grained items or more workers (more lock collisions), a moderate C ≈ 8–64 would be the safer choice.
